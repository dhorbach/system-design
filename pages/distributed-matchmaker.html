Problem

In an online game a player presses Play and expects to be in a match within seconds, with friends on the same side and against opponents of about the same strength (“two friends want a 15-a-side match”, “one player wants a 1 v 1 duel right now”). A matchmaker is the service that decides who plays with whom. The difficulty is that an even match needs enough similar players waiting at the same moment, and often they are not all there yet. The service then has to choose: start a less even match now, or keep people waiting until better-suited players arrive. It has to make that choice for a million players online at once, place every one of them in exactly one match even when machines fail, and let each game define for itself what an even match is.

The examples use one imaginary game. It has three modes: duel (1 v 1), skirmish (5 v 5) and siege (15 v 15). Each player brings three attributes: a class (the role they chose to play, one of five), a combat rating (how strong the equipment they bring is, from 1 to 10) and a skill rating (how good the player is, from 0 to 3,000). The matchmaker itself knows none of the three; only the game’s rules do.

How they relate

A mode has one pool in each region. A pool holds that mode’s waiting tickets in that region. A ticket has 1 to 5 players and is in at most one match at a time. A match has two teams and at most one game server. Counted through: a siege match is 30 players. If four of its tickets are parties (of 3, 3, 2 and 2 players) and the other 20 are single players, the match has 24 tickets.

In the data model a mode is one row of modes per version, a ticket is a row of tickets with its players in ticket_players, and a match is a row of matches with one row of match_tickets per ticket. A pool has no table of its own: it is the tickets that share a region and a mode and are in the state waiting. A rule is one entry in the list a mode keeps in its rules column.

Supporting words

  • Player: a person with an account. A player is on at most one live ticket at a time.
  • Party: the players of one ticket when there is more than one. A party always plays on one team.
  • Team: one side of a match.
  • Attribute: a named fact about a player on a ticket that rules read: a number or a label. The matchmaker stores attributes and never interprets them.
  • Rule set: the rules of one mode, as one list. It has a version, and a match records the version it was formed under.
  • Penalty: the number a rule gives a match: 0 when the match meets the rule, larger the further it is off.
  • Limit: a number in a rule’s settings, such as the 2 in “at most 2 levels”.
  • Game server: the process a match is played on, supplied by the game server fleet.
  • Matchmaker node: one process of the matchmaker. The node that holds a pool’s lease is that pool’s owner, and one pass of an owner over its pool, made once a second, is a round.

Neighbouring words

  • Queue is not used. The waiting tickets of a mode in a region are its pool, and waiting is the state.
  • Request means one HTTP call. A request to join creates a ticket; it is not the ticket.
  • Lobby, battle both mean match here, and only “match” is used. “Game” is kept for the product as a whole, as in “the game’s operators”. Session is kept for a player’s login session.
  • Group, squad mean party.
  • Constraint, filter, criterion mean rule. Score and cost mean penalty.
  • Level is one step of combat rating: a rating of 7 is two levels above a rating of 5.
  • Server on its own always means a game server. A process of the matchmaker is a matchmaker node, and a computer is a machine.
  • Standby is a machine of the ticket store’s database. A matchmaker node that waits to take over a pool is a spare.
  • Reserve is what the assigner does to a game server. Fleet products have their own word for it (Agones allocates a GameServer, Amazon GameLift places a game session); those words are used only for those products’ objects.

Requirements

Each ID reappears as a tag wherever the design implements that requirement.

Functional requirements

  • Join alone or as a party. A player, or a party of up to 5, asks for a match in one mode and gets a ticket back. The largest party is set per mode: 1 in duel, 5 in skirmish and siege. A ticket that could never be matched, because it breaks a rule of its mode all by itself, is refused at once and told which rule (API service).
  • Leave while waiting. A ticket can be cancelled until it is placed in a match. A cancel that arrives after the ticket was placed is refused and answered with the match. It is not forgotten: if that match gets no game server, the ticket is cancelled and does not go back to waiting (correctness).
  • Every player is told the outcome: where to play, or that no match was found. Each player of a matched ticket receives the game server’s address within 2 s of the server being reserved. Any player of a ticket can also ask for its state at any time, including after a reconnect, and gets the same answer (API service).
  • Rules per mode, changed without a release. A mode’s fairness is a rule set that operators change while the system runs. Operators change a rule’s settings, add or remove a rule, or add a mode. Matches formed afterwards follow the new rule set and record its version. A kind of rule the matchmaker does not know yet is added without changing how tickets are stored, matched or assigned (rules).
  • Parties stay together. All players of one ticket play in the same match, on the same team. No rule and no failure splits a party (forming a match).
  • A match comes with a game server. Players are sent to a match only once a game server is reserved for it, in the pool’s region. Until then a status read may show matched with no address. If no server can be reserved within about 10 s, the tickets go back to waiting and keep their waiting time (assigner).

Non-functional requirements

  • One match at a time. No player is ever on two live tickets and no ticket is ever in two matches at once: 0 double placements, whatever crashes. At most one of a ticket’s matches ever gets a game server. A join that is sent again returns the ticket the first one created, whatever state that ticket has reached (ticket store, correctness).
  • Fair matches. Every match meets every rule of its mode’s rule set. In the example game: combat ratings within 2 levels, class counts within 1 between the teams, skill ratings within 100 to 400 points. The rule set is the version in force when the match was formed. The example game’s has five rules: combat ratings in the match span at most 2 levels; for each class the two teams’ counts differ by at most 1; skill ratings span at most a limit that starts at 100 points and grows by 10 points for each second the longest-waiting ticket has waited, up to 400, where a party counts once with its highest rating; the teams’ average skill ratings differ by at most 50 points; and their average combat ratings by at most 0.5 level (rules).
  • Short waits, and always an answer. At the evening peak half of all tickets have a game server within 10 s of joining and 95% within 60 s. At any hour a ticket gets an answer within 300 s, or up to 12 s later when it was placed in a match shortly before then. The answer is a game server’s address or the state expired. A ticket still waiting at 300 s expires. A ticket placed in a match just before then gets that match’s game server within about 10 s, or the match is dropped and the ticket expires in the next round: either way it has its answer within 12 s of the 300 s (matchmaker, assigner, behaviour under load).
  • Accepted means kept. Once a join is accepted, losing any one machine loses no ticket and no waiting time, and matching resumes within 10 s. One exception: losing the main machine of a region’s ticket store pauses joins and matching in that region for up to 60 s. Nothing that was accepted is lost in either case (ticket store).
  • Survive an event start. 8,000 joins per second for 10 s are accepted, with 99% answered within 200 ms. Waits are back inside the evening-peak targets within 60 s of the burst ending (behaviour under load).

Out of scope

  • Working out skill ratings. The design reads a rating that another service computes from past results, and never changes it.
  • Forming a party. Invitations, friends lists and chat happen before a ticket exists; the ticket arrives with its players already listed.
  • The match itself. What happens on the game server, and its networking, starts where this design ends.
  • The game server fleet’s internals. Starting, scaling and health-checking game servers belongs to the fleet. The design uses a small interface to it.
  • Filling empty seats in a match that is already running.
  • Tournaments, private matches and custom rules per match.
  • More than two teams, or teams of unequal size.
  • A confirmation step. A formed match goes straight to a game server; players are not asked to accept it first.
  • Choosing the map, and tickets that name several modes at once.
  • Smaller matches when a pool is thin. A match always fills the mode’s team size; a ticket that cannot be matched expires.
  • Rules that fix the shape of a match before it is filled (for example “3 players at the top level, 5 in the middle, 7 at the bottom”). A rule can state such a shape; the search described here is not built to find it.
  • Matching across regions. A ticket waits in one region’s pool. Each player’s session belongs to one region, and a party forms inside one region.
  • Cheat detection and players who hide their real skill.

Back-of-the-envelope estimates

Before choosing components, size the system: how many tickets arrive, how many wait at once, and how fast players arrive in the emptiest corner of the game. The number that drives the design is in that corner. What the numbers say

  • One owner per pool is enough. The busiest pool has 3,600 tickets waiting and at worst 18,000, which one process reads and works through in a fraction of a second.
  • Never divide the waiting players. With 60 s just to gather 30 players in the emptiest corner, any further split (by node, by skill band, by class) makes waits longer. Every rule is checked inside one pool.
  • Limits must loosen, and waiting must end. When the right players are not there, a limit that widens with waiting finds a looser match, and a ticket that still cannot be matched gets a definite answer.
  • Size for the event start. 8,000 tickets per second is 6 times the evening peak. The components that take joins are sized for it, not for the average.
  • Reserving game servers is a job of its own. About 167 matches a second each need one, from a fleet that can be slow or empty.

High-level design

The design separates four jobs: accepting tickets, keeping them, forming matches and getting each match a game server. Only the ticket store holds state, so every other part can crash and restart without losing a ticket.

The life of one ticket

  1. JoinedThree friends press Play for a siege match in Europe. The API reads their attributes from the profile service and stores one ticket, waiting.
  2. ReadWithin a second, the matchmaker node that owns the pool (Europe, siege) reads all of its waiting tickets afresh.
  3. FormedStarting from the ticket that has waited longest, it gathers the nearest tickets that every rule allows, 30 players in all, and splits them into two teams.
  4. CommittedOne write stores the match and moves its 24 tickets to matched, but only if every one of them is still waiting.
  5. ReservedAn assigner asks the fleet for a game server, then marks the match ready and its tickets assigned. If no server comes within 10 s, the tickets go back to waiting and keep their waiting time.
  6. ToldThe API holds an open connection to each waiting player. It sees the change within half a second and sends the three friends the server’s address.

Clients use one small REST API to join, to leave and to be told the outcome. A join is answered only once the ticket is committed to the ticket store, and it is the store, not the API’s memory, that refuses a second live ticket for the same player.

Every player of a ticket is told the outcome through the stream: the game server’s address, or that the ticket was cancelled or expired. The API node that holds a stream does not wait to be told: twice a second it asks the store about all the tickets it is watching, so no outcome can be lost with a message.

What each call guarantees

  • Join answers only after the ticket and its players are committed together, so an accepted ticket is durable. The API first reads each player’s attributes from the profile service and checks that the ticket could ever be matched: the party is within the mode’s limit, every attribute the rules read is present, and the ticket breaks no rule by itself.
  • Join again is safe. The client makes up a join key and sends it with the join. A join that arrives again with the same key, because the first answer was lost, is answered with the ticket the first one created, in whatever state that ticket is by now, also when it already has a game server. A join with a new key from a player who is live on another ticket is refused.
  • Leave cancels the ticket if it is still waiting. If a match took it first, the cancel is refused and answered with that match, and the request is kept on the ticket: should that match be dropped for want of a game server, the ticket is cancelled and does not go back into the pool.
  • Read and the stream give the same facts: the state, and once the ticket is assigned, the match, the team and the game server’s address. Only a player on the ticket may ask.
  • Write a mode stores a new version of its rule set after checking it against the rule library. Matches formed from the next round on follow it.
  • Read a match returns its state, its two teams and, once it has one, its game server. A player may read the match their own ticket is in; the game server and support tools read any match with credentials of their own.
  • Read the modes and list the rule kinds are for operators: the current version of every mode with its rule set, and the kinds and settings a rule set may use.

How a player is told

The client opens the stream right after joining and keeps it open: a long-lived HTTP response (server-sent events) or a WebSocket, whichever the game already uses. One API node may hold tens of thousands of streams. Every 500 ms it reads the state of all the tickets it is watching in one query and sends each stream what changed. A player therefore has the server’s address at most about 0.5 s after it is stored, inside the 2 s of FR3, and the store is asked about 2 times a second per API node, not once per player.

Nothing depends on the stream. A client that lost it asks for its ticket by id, or without the id through GET /v1/players/me/ticket, and opens a new one.

When a player disappears

A client that quits cleanly leaves through the API. A client that crashes or loses its connection leaves nothing behind but a closed stream, and a closed stream proves little: the players of a party can hold streams on different API nodes, and a player who reconnects usually lands on another node. No API node can tell from its own streams that nobody is listening any more, so none of them cancels a ticket on that evidence. The ticket stays in its pool until it is matched or expires, and what a match does with a player who is not there is the game’s business.

A matchmaker that needs such tickets gone sooner has to put the fact “someone is still listening” into the store, for example as a last-seen time per ticket that the stream checks refresh, and pay for one more write per waiting ticket every few seconds. This design does not.

Two unique indexes can refuse this transaction (ticket store). The stream check

SELECT t.id, t.state, t.match_id, t.team, t.end_reason, m.server_address   -- every 500 ms, per API node
FROM tickets t LEFT JOIN matches m ON m.id = t.match_id
WHERE t.id IN (?, ?, ?);

The list holds every ticket the node has a stream for. A stream ends when its ticket reaches assigned, cancelled or expired.

Answers

  • Join. 201 for a new ticket. 200 for the same join key again. 409 already_live when a listed player is live on another ticket (waiting, or matched and not yet assigned), with that ticket’s id. 422 for a party over the mode’s limit, an unknown or disabled mode, a player without an attribute the rule set reads (missing_attribute), or a ticket that breaks a rule by itself (rule_refused, with the rule). 503 with Retry-After when the profile service does not answer within 100 ms or the store cannot be reached; no ticket was created.
  • Leave. 200 when cancelled, also when it already was. 409 matched with the match_id when a match took the ticket first; the request to leave is recorded on the ticket.
  • Write a mode. 201 with the new version. 422 when a rule names a kind that is not in the library, lacks a setting or gives one of the wrong type.

Limits on the caller

  • Who may join for whom. The caller must be one of the listed players. How the others agreed to be listed is the business of whatever forms parties, which is out of scope here.
  • Retries. After a 503 the client waits with backoff and jitter, spread over 10 s, so that a region coming back from an outage is not met by every client in the same second (behaviour under load).
  • Connections to the store. All API nodes of a region together use at most 128 connections for writes. With too few, joins queue behind each other: in the measurement the slowest 1% of joins took 27.6 ms on 128 connections and over 200 ms on 32.

2Ticket store

The ticket store is the single source of truth: every other component can crash and pick up from it, and a join it has acknowledged survives the loss of any one machine.

Each region has its own store: a relational database on three machines, in which a commit is acknowledged only once a second machine holds it too. A ticket moves through states: waiting → matched → assigned. A waiting ticket can instead end as cancelled or expired, and a matched ticket whose match gets no game server goes back to waiting. Every one of these moves is a single write that carries its own condition (“move it only if it is still in the state I expect”), so of two writers that want the same ticket only one can win.

Why a relational database, and why three machines

The design needs three things from its store: a commit that survives a machine, a write that changes a row only if the row is still in a given state, and a rule that one player has at most one live ticket. A relational database gives all three as ordinary features, so the guarantees can be read off the statements. PostgreSQL is the database this guide’s statements are written for.

Brief: synchronous standby. A standby is a database machine that receives every change as it happens. With synchronous commit, the main machine (the primary) answers “committed” only after a standby has the change on disk too. If the primary is lost, a standby has everything that was ever acknowledged.

The store runs a primary and two standbys, each in its own zone, and a commit waits for whichever standby answers first. Two standbys are there so that losing one machine, whichever it is, never leaves a commit waiting for a machine that is gone. The price is time: every commit waits for two disks. It is paid once per join and three times per match, and behaviour under load shows that it does not limit throughput.

One live ticket per player

A player’s row in ticket_players is live while its ticket is waiting or matched. The store keeps a unique index on player_id that covers only live rows, so a second live row for the same player cannot be inserted, whichever API node tries and however the two joins are timed. Every write that ends a ticket (assigned, cancelled, expired) clears live on its players in the same transaction, which is what lets the player join again. A second unique index, on the creator and the join key, recognises a join that is sent twice.

Surviving the loss of a machine

Losing a standby changes nothing a client can see: commits wait for the other one. Losing the primary stops the region’s store until a standby is promoted, which this design allows up to 60 s for. The failover manager promotes the standby that has received more of the primary’s log: every acknowledged commit reached at least one of the two, so the one that is further ahead has all of them. The other standby is then pointed at the new primary. During that time joins are answered with “try again”, owners form nothing and assigners reserve nothing. Every ticket acknowledged before the loss is on the new primary, with its waiting time, and the new primary still has a standby to wait for, so nothing accepted afterwards sits on one disk only. The lost machine is replaced without hurry. This is the one exception in NFR4.

The write that ends a ticket

WHERE id = ? AND state = 'matched';

If this write changes no row either, the match was readied or dropped in between. The assigner’s drop reads the mark (assigner). end_reason is player for a cancelled ticket, with cancelled_by holding the player’s id; for an expired ticket it is ttl, mode_disabled or rules_changed. A dropped match records drop_reason: no_server or fleet_error.

Details that matter

  • Ids ordered by time. Ticket and match ids are version 7 UUIDs, whose first bits are a timestamp, so new rows land at the end of the key’s index. PostgreSQL 18 generates them with uuidv7(); older versions get them from the API. The examples show short ids such as t_41 for readability.
  • Removing history. Rows are kept for 7 days. A scheduled job deletes older ones in small batches: in the busiest region about 200 tickets a second on average, with their players, placements and matches, 675 rows a second. That is small next to the 16,500 row writes a second of an event start.
  • The database’s clock. Every statement that compares a time uses now(), which in PostgreSQL is the start time of the current transaction on the database machine. No decision depends on the clock of an API node, a matchmaker node or an assigner.
  • Measured throughput. On the prototype described under capacity estimates, this schema carried the whole life of a ticket (join, match, claim, result) at 8,168 tickets a second on 128 connections and 15,280 on 256, against 2,400 needed at an event start. Joins held at 2,400 a second were answered in 9.5 ms at the median and 27.6 ms for the slowest 1%.
  • The setting. In PostgreSQL the two standbys are named in synchronous_standby_names as ANY 1 (…), with synchronous_commit = on: a commit then waits for any one of them, which is why a failover has to promote the standby that is further ahead. The measurement ran one standby; waiting for the faster of two is no slower.
  • Another database. MySQL 8 has no index with a WHERE. The usual substitute for the unique index is a generated column that holds the player’s id while the row is live and NULL otherwise, with a unique index on it; a unique index there allows many NULLs.

3Matchmaker

The matchmaker turns each pool’s waiting tickets into matches, once a second. Several matchmaker nodes must run so that one can die. The difficulty is that two nodes working on the same pool would keep picking the same tickets, and all but one of them would work for nothing.

The answer is one owner per pool, held by a lease: a lock that expires unless its holder renews it. The owner keeps nothing between rounds. Every second it reads the pool’s waiting tickets afresh, forms matches in memory and commits them, so a node that takes over a pool starts from exactly what the store says. The lease is there for speed only. If two nodes ever believe they own the same pool, the condition in the match commit lets only one of them take each ticket.

Why the obvious designs fail

  • One matchmaker node is a single point of failure: while it is down, nobody in the region is matched.
  • Every node works on every pool. The nodes read the same tickets and form overlapping matches. In a test with two such workers, 135 of the second worker’s 135 siege matches shared a ticket with one of the first worker’s and had to be thrown away. The table under “One owner against racing workers” has the full comparison.

The lease

Brief: lease. A lock with an expiry time. The holder must renew it before it expires; if the holder dies, the lease runs out by itself and another node can take it. Unlike a plain lock, a crashed holder cannot block everyone for ever.

Each pool has one row in the store’s pool_leases table: its owner and the time the lease expires. The row is created together with the first version of the mode. The owner renews every 2 s for another 6 s. The other matchmaker nodes are spares: once a second each checks for pools whose lease has run out and tries to take one. A region has three pools (one per mode) and three or more matchmaker nodes, so every pool has an owner and a spare.

takeover after an owner dies: 6 s + 1 s + 1 s = 8 s

The three terms are the lease running out, the spare’s next check and the new owner’s first round. That is inside the 10 s of NFR4, and no ticket is touched: the dead owner held nothing that the store does not have.

Why the lease needs no fencing

A node can be paused (a long garbage collection, a frozen virtual machine) and wake up after its lease passed to another node, still believing it owns the pool. Many designs must then reject its writes with a fencing token, a number that grows each time ownership changes. This design does not need one, because being the owner is never what makes a write valid. A match commit is valid only if every one of its tickets is still waiting, and that is checked by the write itself.

One round

  1. Read the rules. The owner reads the newest version of the mode’s rule set.
  2. Read the pool. One query returns every waiting ticket of the pool, oldest first, with its size, sort key and summary. Tickets whose summary was built for an older version of the rule set are rebuilt first, at most 500 in a round; the rest of them sit this round out.
  3. Expire. Tickets older than the mode’s time limit (300 s) are moved to expired, with the usual condition that they are still waiting.
  4. Look at the fleet’s backlog. If more than 2,000 of the pool’s matches are still reserving, the owner forms nothing this round, so that a slow fleet shows up as waiting and not as matches dropped wholesale.
  5. Form. The owner forms as many matches as the rules allow, in memory, starting from the oldest ticket (forming a match). It gives itself 500 ms; what it has not reached by then waits for the next round.
  6. Commit. Each match is one transaction. The matches of one round share no ticket, so the owner commits them side by side on 16 connections.

Each is one statement, so each is its own transaction and now() is the store’s clock. Renewal runs on its own timer and its own connection, so a long round cannot let the lease run out.

When a round goes wrong

  • The round cannot finish in its second (a backlog after an outage, a costly rule set). Forming stops at its 500 ms deadline, the owner commits what it has and starts the next round on time. Tickets are taken oldest first, so the ones left over are the newest. With forming at its deadline and the worst pool, a round takes 56 + 50 + 500 + 469 = 1,075 ms. Rounds never overlap, so the next one starts 75 ms late.
  • The rule set changed while tickets waited. A ticket’s mode_version says which version its summary was built for. When it is behind, the owner reads the ticket’s players, builds the summary and the sort key again and stores them, on the condition that the ticket is still waiting with the old version. It does this before forming and outside the 500 ms, for at most 500 tickets a round, so a pool of 3,600 is through in 8 rounds; a ticket not yet rebuilt is left out of forming until it is. If the players lack an attribute the new rules read, the ticket is expired with end_reason = 'rules_changed'. An owner that keeps decoded summaries between rounds must key them by ticket and version.
  • The owner runs an older build that does not know a kind of rule in the newest version. It cannot judge tickets built for that version, so it releases the pool’s lease and raises an alert. A node checks that it can load a pool’s newest rule set before it claims that pool, so the released lease goes to a node on a newer build and not to another old one. If no node can load the version, the pool forms nothing and its tickets expire at 300 s, each with a definite answer.
  • A rule fails while judging (a fault in a new kind’s code). The search for that one match is abandoned and counted, and the others go on. An alert fires for any pool that has tickets waiting and formed no match for 10 s.

When one owner is not enough

With the example rule set, forming the worst pool uses 63 ms of a 500 ms allowance: 8 times the room it needs. A rule set can cost far more; one that demands teams exactly equal at every combat rating level took 378 ms for 14,400 tickets, which is about 470 ms for the worst pool of 18,000. The steps, in the order they would be taken:

  1. The deadline. Nothing changes until forming reaches 500 ms. Past that the round still ends on time and the newest tickets wait one round more.
  2. More cores, same owner. The owner cuts the pool’s tickets, already sorted by sort key, into disjoint chunks and forms each chunk on its own core. Chunks share no ticket, so two cores can never pick the same one. On every other round the cuts move by half a chunk, so a ticket next to a cut is not stuck there. A pool is cut only while each chunk still holds at least 2,000 tickets, so a thin pool is never divided. Measured at 100,000 tickets: 8 chunks take 61 ms instead of 269 ms and match 99.3% of the players in the round instead of 99.4%.
  3. More machines. The lease is taken per chunk instead of per pool, with the chunk’s bounds stored in its lease row. Nothing else changes, because safety never came from the lease. It is not built here: nothing in this design’s numbers needs it.

Tuning

  • Lease length trades takeover time against false takeovers. 6 s with a renewal every 2 s survives two lost renewals. A shorter lease takes over sooner and hands pools back and forth when the store is slow.
  • Round length sets the shortest possible wait. One second is a floor on waiting that players do not notice and leaves the store one read per pool per second.
  • Commit connections per owner: 16 covers the worst pool in 469 ms. More helps only if the store has the connections to give.

Inside the matchmaker: forming a match

Forming a match means choosing, from thousands of waiting tickets, a set that exactly fills two teams and breaks no rule. Trying every combination is out of the question, so the engine builds one match at a time around the ticket that has waited longest.

It adds the nearest tickets that every rule lets in (in the example game, nearest in skill rating) until the player count is exact. It places them on two teams, each ticket whole, so a party is never split. Then it swaps tickets between the teams for as long as a swap brings the match closer to meeting every rule. A match is committed only when every rule is met. If the engine finds none, the ticket waits for the next round, when limits that widen with waiting give it more candidates. The engine can miss a match that exists, but it never forms one that breaks a rule.

Computed once, at the join

The engine never looks at single players. When a ticket is created, the rule library reduces its players’ attributes to three things, stored on the ticket: its size (how many players), its sort_key (its value of the attribute the mode sorts by; in the example game the highest skill rating among its players) and its summary (for each attribute the lowest value, the highest and the sum, or a count per value). A party is one entry with one summary, which is what keeps it together. The three stay as they are for as long as the mode’s rule set does; when operators change the rule set, the owner computes them again.

The four steps

  1. Anchor. Take the longest-waiting ticket that is not yet in a match this round. Its waiting time sets every limit that widens.
  2. Fill. Walk outwards from the anchor along the sort key, nearest first. Add a ticket when every rule says it can still fit and the players do not exceed two teams. Stop when the count is exact.
  3. Split. Place the tickets on two teams, largest tickets first, each ticket whole.
  4. Swap. While some rule still gives a penalty, exchange the two tickets of equal size, one from each team, that lower the total penalty most. Commit at 0.

If the swaps end above 0, the engine drops the ticket it added last, takes the next candidate and tries again, a bounded number of times. Then it gives up on this anchor for this round and moves to the next-oldest ticket.

How a limit widens

A limit in a rule can be a fixed number or one that grows with the anchor’s waiting time. The example game’s skill limit starts at 100 points and grows by 10 points a second up to 400:

after 12 s: 100 + 10 × 12 = 220 points
reaches its cap after: (400 − 100) ÷ 10 = 30 s

So a ticket in a busy part of the pool is matched in its first round against players within 100 points, and a ticket with few neighbours trades evenness for time, by a known amount per second, and stops trading at 400. The combat rating limit never widens: a difference of more than 2 levels is not a match the example game wants at any waiting time. A ticket that is still unmatched at 300 s expires.

Why the oldest ticket goes first

Starting every match from the longest-waiting ticket has three effects. Nobody is overtaken for ever by newer tickets. The widest limits are applied where they are needed, around the ticket that earned them. And when a round runs out of time, the tickets it did not reach are the newest ones.

The engine

// One round over a pool, abridged. tickets is sorted by sort key.
func round(tickets []Ticket, rules []Rule, teamSize int, deadline time.Time) []Match {
	used := make([]bool, len(tickets))
	var out []Match
	for _, a := range oldestFirst(tickets) {
		if used[a] || time.Now().After(deadline) {
			continue
		}
		wait := tickets[a].Wait.Seconds()
		sel := newSelection(a, tickets[a])
		next := nearestFirst(tickets, a, used) // walks outwards from the anchor
		for tries := 0; tries <= 8; tries++ {
			for sel.Players < 2*teamSize {
				c, ok := next()
				if !ok {
					break
				}
				if sel.Players+tickets[c].Players <= 2*teamSize && fitsAll(rules, sel, tickets[c], wait) {
					sel.Add(c, tickets[c])
				}
			}
			if sel.Players < 2*teamSize {
				break // too few candidates: the anchor waits for the next round
			}
			if m, ok := split(sel, rules, teamSize, wait); ok {
				for _, i := range sel.Tickets {
					used[i] = true
				}
				out = append(out, m)
				break
			}
			sel.DropLast() // replace the last-added ticket by the next candidate
		}
	}
	return out
}

func split(sel *Selection, rules []Rule, teamSize int, wait float64) (Match, bool) {
	a, b, ok := placeLargestFirst(sel, teamSize) // each ticket whole: a party is never split
	if !ok {
		return Match{}, false
	}
	p := penalty(rules, sel, a, b, wait)
	for swaps := 0; swaps < 40 && p > 0; swaps++ {
		i, j, q := bestSwap(rules, sel, a, b, wait) // equal-sized tickets, one from each team
		if q >= p {
			break
		}
		a.Swap(i, b, j)
		p = q
	}
	return Match{TeamA: a, TeamB: b}, p == 0
}

The engine names no attribute and no game. duel runs through the same code with a team size of 1 and a rule set that has no team rules; nothing in the engine is special for it. The search (fill, split, swap) is one step that can be replaced: given the candidates and the rules, return a match with penalty 0 or nothing.

Bounds

  • At most 40 swaps per split and 8 replacements per anchor, so one anchor cannot use up the round.
  • A deadline of 500 ms for forming in a round (matchmaker).
  • No match above penalty 0 is ever returned. The bounds can make the engine miss a match; they cannot make it break a rule.

Inside the matchmaker: rules

Rules are the part every game changes, so they are kept apart from the part no game changes. The matchmaker knows a ticket’s size and how long it has waited, and nothing else about its players. Everything a game means by a fair match is a rule.

A rule answers one question about a finished match: how far is it from acceptable? The answer is a penalty, 0 when the match meets the rule. A rule never says how to build a match, so the tenth rule is as easy to add as the first. A mode’s rule set is a list of rules with their settings, and operators change it while the system runs. A rule is data: a kind, the attribute it reads and its settings, listed in a mode’s rule set. A kind fulfils a contract of three functions.

penaltyThe totals of the whole match and of each team, the team size, the waiting timeA number: 0 when the match meets the rule, larger the further it is offRequired. The same input always gives the same answer

The engine commits a match only when every rule’s penalty is 0, so a match that breaks a rule cannot be formed, whatever the search does. The waiting time handed to a rule is that of the match’s longest-waiting ticket. A limit written with a start, a step and a cap is read as the start plus the step for every second waited, up to the cap; that is how any rule of any kind loosens with waiting.

What a rule is allowed to see

A rule reads totals and nothing else: for each number attribute the lowest value, the highest and the sum; for each label attribute a count per value; the number of players, and how many of them came in a party. For a number that a rule reads once per ticket, a party counting as its highest value, the totals also keep the lowest and the highest of those per-ticket values. The engine keeps totals for each ticket, for the tickets selected so far and for each team. A rule cannot read another ticket, the store or a clock. Two things follow. Judging a swap means adjusting two teams’ totals, which costs the same for teams of 5 and teams of 15. And a rule can be tested on its own, with made-up totals.

The check at the join

A ticket that breaks a rule all by itself can never be in a match. The API therefore asks every rule’s fits about the new ticket alone, with the waiting time set to the mode’s time limit so that every limit is at its widest. In the example game a party whose combat ratings are 3 levels apart is refused at once and told why, where it would otherwise wait 300 s for nothing.

Changing a rule set while tickets wait

A mode’s rule set is versioned: writing a mode adds a row and changes none. Before it is stored, the new version goes through each kind’s check, and a rule set that names an unknown kind or leaves out a setting is refused. Owners read the newest version at the start of every round, so the change takes effect within a second and no matchmaker node is restarted. Each match records the version it was formed under. A waiting ticket built under the older version has its summary rebuilt by the owner within the next few rounds. If the new rule set reads an attribute its players do not have, the ticket expires with the reason rules_changed, and its players may join again.

A new attribute needs no release at all: the profile service starts returning it and a rule set names it. A new kind of rule does need a release, in this order: the matchmaker nodes, then the API nodes, and only then a rule set that uses the kind. An API node that meets a rule set it cannot load refuses joins for that mode with “try again” and raises an alert. The new kind changes nothing in how tickets are stored, matched or assigned.

Where the idea comes from

The team behind World of Tanks Blitz, a mobile game with 7 players a side, describe arriving at the same contract. Their balancer once paired players up and then repaired the teams in passes, one pass per requirement. Each new pass had to avoid undoing the earlier ones, and they call that road a dead end. They replaced it with one evaluation function that adds a penalty for every requirement a candidate pair of teams breaks, so that a new requirement became one more term in the function.

Their search is different from the one in this guide. It is simulated annealing: start from two empty teams, make a random move (add, remove, replace or swap a player), keep the move or not by a probability that falls over time, and stop at penalty 0. This guide’s search starts from the longest-waiting ticket and takes the nearest tickets first, because that serves tickets oldest first and bounds the work per match; random moves give neither. Their search is the natural replacement for the search step when a rule set defeats the greedy one. They also note that a rule about who may meet at all is better placed where candidates are picked, which is what fits is for.

Two kinds in Go

// A rule judges; it never builds.
type Rule interface {
	// Fits reports whether a match containing the selection and t could still meet the rule.
	Fits(sel, t *Totals, wait float64) bool
	// Penalty is 0 when the match meets the rule, and grows with the distance.
	Penalty(match, a, b *Totals, teamSize int, wait float64) float64
}

// Limit is a setting that may widen while the longest-waiting ticket waits.
type Limit struct{ Start, Step, Cap float64 }

func (l Limit) At(wait float64) float64 { return math.Min(l.Cap, l.Start+l.Step*wait) }

// Span: the values of one number attribute across the match differ by at most Max.
type Span struct {
	Attribute string
	Max       Limit
}

func (r Span) Fits(sel, t *Totals, wait float64) bool {
	lo := math.Min(sel.Min[r.Attribute], t.Min[r.Attribute])
	hi := math.Max(sel.Max[r.Attribute], t.Max[r.Attribute])
	return hi-lo <= r.Max.At(wait)
}

func (r Span) Penalty(m, a, b *Totals, teamSize int, wait float64) float64 {
	return math.Max(0, m.Max[r.Attribute]-m.Min[r.Attribute]-r.Max.At(wait))
}

// Mirror: for every value of an attribute the two teams' counts differ by at most MaxDiff.
type Mirror struct {
	Attribute string
	MaxDiff   int
}

func (r Mirror) Fits(sel, t *Totals, wait float64) bool { return true }

func (r Mirror) Penalty(m, a, b *Totals, teamSize int, wait float64) float64 {
	p := 0
	for value := range m.Count[r.Attribute] {
		d := a.Count[r.Attribute][value] - b.Count[r.Attribute][value]
		if d < 0 {
			d = -d
		}
		if d > r.MaxDiff {
			p += d - r.MaxDiff
		}
	}
	return float64(p)
}

The listing leaves out the setting that lets a party count once: with it, Span reads the lowest and the highest of the tickets’ highest values where it now reads the lowest and the highest of all players. A fixed limit such as “2 levels” is a Limit whose start and cap are equal.

What the contract costs, and what it cannot say

  • Cost. Calling rules through the contract takes about 5 times as long as the same limits written straight into the engine: 48 ms against 10 ms for 14,400 tickets. At this design’s sizes that is 50 ms of a second.
  • A rule sees totals, not players. “These two players must not meet” or “not at the bottom of the list three matches running” needs a new total in the engine, or a new attribute from the profile service.
  • The search is greedy. A rule set whose matches are rare among the nearest tickets is found slowly or missed. A rule that fixes the shape of a match before it is filled, such as a template of how many players stand at each level, would need a fits that knows the shape, or another search.
  • Trying a rule set. Because a rule reads only totals, a rule set can be tried without a store or a network: generate tickets with the game’s mix of attributes, run rounds with arrivals, and read the time per round, the share matched and the waits. The figures in this guide were produced that way.

4Assigner

The assigner gets every match a game server. Forming a match and reserving a server are two writes in two systems, so a crash can fall between them.

The match is committed first and the server is reserved second. A committed match without a server is visible in the store, so any assigner can pick it up: recovery after a crash is the same loop as normal work. The reservation carries the match’s id, so asking twice returns the same server. A deadline keeps players from waiting on a fleet that has no server to give: after 10 s the match is dropped and its tickets go back to waiting, first in line, with their waiting time intact.

Why the obvious orders fail

  • Reserve first, then commit. If the commit then fails, because a ticket was cancelled in between or the matchmaker crashed, a game server sits reserved for a match that does not exist. And a slow fleet would stall the owner’s round, which has a second to finish.
  • Reserve inside the commit’s transaction. The transaction would hold its tickets locked for the length of a network call to another system.

The loop

Four assigners run in each region. Every 200 ms each of them claims a batch of matches, and it works on all the matches it has claimed at the same time, each until it is ready or dropped. An assigner keeps nothing that the store does not have: if it dies, its claims run out and another assigner takes its matches.

  1. Claim. Take up to 50 matches that are reserving and that nobody has claimed in the last 5 s, and mark them as claimed. Two assigners never take the same match in the same pass.
  2. Reserve. Ask the fleet for a game server for the match, in the pool’s region. The call names the match’s id, and the fleet answers a repeated call with the server it already gave. The call is given 2 s, less than the claim lasts.
  3. Record. Store the server’s address, move the match to ready and its tickets to assigned, in one transaction that requires the match to be still reserving.
  4. Or try again. If the fleet has no server or does not answer, wait (200 ms, doubling to 1.6 s), renew the claim for another 5 s and ask again while the deadline allows. The longest wait and a call together take 3.6 s, so a claim never runs out under an assigner that is alive.
  5. Or drop. Once a match’s deadline has passed, any assigner drops it, whoever holds the claim: the match moves to dropped and its tickets back to waiting, again only if the match is still reserving. A ticket whose player asked to leave in the meantime is cancelled instead.
Brief: idempotent. A call is idempotent when making it twice has the same effect as making it once. “Reserve a server for match m_7” is idempotent if the fleet remembers which server it gave to m_7; “reserve a server” is not.

The claim is only a courtesy between assigners, like the pool’s lease between owners: it stops two of them calling the fleet for the same match at the same moment. If an assigner dies with a claim, the claim runs out after 5 s and another one takes the match. What keeps the outcome single is the condition in the two final writes: a match can leave reserving only once.

The deadline and the way back

A match’s deadline is set when it is committed: 10 s later. Dropping a match is not a failure of the system; it is the planned answer to a fleet that is full. The tickets return to their pool with their original starting time, so the next round takes them before anyone who joined later, and their limits are as wide as their whole wait has earned. Players see “match found” turn back into “searching”, which is why the server’s address is sent only once the server exists.

A ticket whose 300 s run out while it sits in a match that is then dropped is expired by the next round. The drop comes within one pass of an assigner after the deadline, and the round within a second of the drop:

latest expiry after the 300 s: 10 s + 0.2 s + 1 s = 11.2 s

NFR3 rounds this up to 12 s.

A slow answer from the fleet can arrive after another assigner has dropped the match.

FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED makes each assigner pass over rows another assigner is claiming at that instant, so they share the work without waiting on each other (PostgreSQL 9.5 and later, MySQL 8.0 and later). A match past its deadline is found through the same index as the claim, with reserve_deadline < now() in place of the claim’s condition. In “ready” and in “drop”, the first statement decides: if it changes no row, the assigner rolls back and does nothing more to the store. After a lost “ready” it frees the reservation. The drop decides each ticket in a single statement: back to waiting, or cancelled if its player asked to leave. A leave that is being recorded at that very moment either is seen by the drop or finds the ticket no longer matched, and then the API repeats the cancel on the waiting ticket. A dropped match keeps its rows in match_tickets, so the history shows which tickets it held. A failed call to the fleet is written to last_error.

The fleet’s interface

  • PUT /reservations/{match_id} with the region, the mode and the two teams’ player ids returns a game server’s address. Repeating it for the same match id returns the same server. It answers 503 when no server is free.
  • DELETE /reservations/{match_id} frees the server and can be repeated.
  • The fleet frees by itself a reserved server that no player has connected to after 60 s. This is a property the design asks of the fleet.

Fleet products provide the reservation itself, under their own names: in Agones a GameServerAllocation moves a ready GameServer to allocated and returns its address and ports; Amazon GameLift places a game session. The three properties above (the same server for the same match id, a free call that can be repeated, release after 60 s without players) are not all built into them. They come from a thin adapter in front of the product. For Agones, for example, the adapter labels the game server with the match id when it allocates and looks for that label before it allocates again, and the game server process shuts itself down when nobody connects.

When things are down

  • The fleet is down or full. Matches are retried until their deadlines and then dropped. Tickets go round (matched, dropped, matched again) and keep their waiting time; if the fleet stays down they expire at 300 s with a definite answer. So that this does not churn the whole pool, an owner stops forming for a pool while more than 2,000 of its matches are reserving.
  • An assigner dies after a drop, before freeing the server. The fleet’s own 60 s rule frees it. One server stands idle for up to a minute.
  • Every assigner is down. Tickets stay matched; no player is told anything false. The first assigner back drops every match past its deadline and the tickets wait again with their waiting time. The signal to alert on is the age of the oldest reserving match.

Even if every match of the burst waited for its server, they would all be served 3 s later, well inside the 60 s of NFR5. The cap of 2,000 reserving matches per pool is two seconds of that rate. Each match costs the store three commits when the first reservation succeeds (its own, the claim and the result), plus one renewal for every retry. Correctness: one ticket, one match

The system promises that a ticket is never in two matches at once and that a player is never on two live tickets, whatever crashes and whatever races. Neither promise rests on a component behaving well. Both rest on writes that carry their own condition.

Every race in the design has the same shape. Two writers want the same ticket: two owners of one pool, a match and a player who leaves, a match and the time limit. Each write says “only if the ticket is still waiting”. The store applies them one after the other, and the second finds nothing to change. A match takes all of its tickets or none: if one is gone, the whole match is undone and the others stay waiting.

Brief: guarded write. A write that names the state it expects to find, in the same statement that changes it. The store reports how many rows it changed, so a writer that changed fewer than it asked for knows it lost and stops.

A crash between two writes

The design has two places where one job spans two writes. Between the match commit and the reservation, the stored match is in the state reserving, which is exactly what assigners look for, so whoever crashed, some assigner continues. Between the reservation and its record, the next assigner repeats the reservation with the same match id and receives the same server. In both cases the end state is ready, or dropped at the deadline; nothing is left half done, and the only thing repeated is a call that is safe to repeat.

A join that is sent twice

An API node can crash after the join committed and before it answered. The client cannot tell whether its ticket exists, so it sends the join again, with the same join key. The store refuses a second ticket with that creator and key, and the API answers with the first one in whatever state it has reached by then: still waiting, or already holding a game server’s address. One ticket exists and its waiting time started with the first attempt. A client that has lost its key as well asks for its most recent ticket before it joins anew.

What the system does not promise

  • That the first match found is played. A ticket can be in a match that is dropped and later in another. It is never in two at once, and at most one of its matches ever gets a game server.
  • That every player shows up. A player whose client crashed stays on the ticket until it is matched or expires. What a match does with an absent player is the game’s business.
  • That a player in a match cannot join again. A ticket stops being live when it gets its game server, so its players can create a new ticket while that match is played. Keeping a player out of two matches at once is the game’s business too.
  • The best possible match. Every match meets every rule. Among the matches that do, the search takes the first it finds.

The rarer paths

  • A server reserved for nobody. An assigner that loses the race in the table frees its reservation. If it dies before it can, the fleet frees a server nobody connected to after 60 s. Loss: one server idle for up to a minute.
  • A ticket whose player vanished. Nothing cancels it, for the reason given under the API service. It is matched like any other, or expires at 300 s. Loss: possibly a match with an absent player.
  • Joining again after the end. When a ticket is cancelled or expired, its players are no longer live, so their next join creates a new ticket with a new id and a new starting time. Waiting time is not carried over, by design: the old ticket had its answer.
  • A ticket that outlives its rule set. A ticket built under version 3 is still waiting when version 4 arrives. The owner rebuilds its summary under a guard (still waiting, still version 3), and a match commit requires each ticket to be still waiting at the version the owner read, so a rebuild and a match commit cannot both win. If version 4 reads an attribute the ticket’s players do not have, the ticket expires with the reason rules_changed, and the player may join again.
  • A mode switched off. Joins for it are refused, and the pool’s owner expires its waiting tickets with the reason mode_disabled, through the same guarded write as the time limit.
  • A stale owner’s expiry. An owner that lost its lease may still run the expiry statement once. It changes only tickets that are waiting and truly past their limit by the store’s clock, so the worst it can do is what the new owner would have done.

Behaviour under load

A matchmaker has two bad moments, and they are opposites. At an event start a tenth of the players online join within 10 seconds: 8,000 tickets per second, 6 times the evening peak. At the quietest hour the thinnest pool has too few players to fill a match at all.

The burst is the easier one. A join is one small commit, and the store commits thousands of them a second because commits that wait at the same moment are written together. The owner of a flooded pool forms matches oldest first and stops at a deadline inside each second, so a backlog delays the newest tickets and nothing else. The thin pool has no machinery to save it. There, limits that widen with waiting find a looser match, and a ticket that still cannot be matched gets a definite answer, expired, after 300 s.

Why the store is not the limit

Every commit waits for two machines to write it to disk, which takes about 10 ms. It would be natural to conclude that one connection’s 100 commits a second is all there is. It is not, because a database writes its log once for all the commits that are waiting at that moment.

Brief: group commit. A database makes a commit durable by writing its log to disk. When several transactions commit at the same moment, one write to disk covers all of them. So the time for one commit stays the same as more connections commit, and the number of commits per second grows with the number of connections.

The busiest region receives 2,400 joins a second at an event start. At 10 ms each, that keeps 24 connections busy; the API has 128. Measured on the prototype described under capacity estimates, 128 connections carried 12,333 joins a second, and joins held at 2,400 a second were answered within 27.6 ms for 99% of them, against the 200 ms that NFR5 allows.

A join also waits for the profile service, which the design requires to answer 10,000 lookups a second, 99% of them within 50 ms. Added to the 27.6 ms of the commit, that is about 78 ms for 99% of joins. A lookup that takes longer than 100 ms ends the join with “try again”, and such joins count against NFR5: the matchmaker cannot accept players it knows nothing about.

Who waits in a burst

  • Oldest first. Every match starts from the longest-waiting ticket, so a burst cannot push earlier tickets back.
  • A deadline inside the round. An owner stops forming at 500 ms and commits what it has. The worst round, with 18,000 tickets waiting, takes 638 ms, so the next round starts on time.
  • Commits side by side. An owner commits a round’s matches on 16 connections: 1,600 a second where one connection manages 100.
  • A cap on matches without a server. A burst of matches is also a burst of 300 reservations a second in the busiest region. While more than 2,000 of a pool’s matches are still reserving, its owner forms no more, so a slow fleet shows up as waiting, not as matches dropped wholesale.
  • Clients that spread out. A client that is told to try again waits a random time before it does, so a region that comes back is not hit by everyone in the same second.

What a thin pool can be given

In the emptiest corner the 30 players of a siege match take 60 s to arrive, and no component can make players exist. What the design can do is not waste them: one pool per mode and region, never divided further; limits that widen, so that the players who are there become a match sooner; and an honest end at 300 s. What operators can do is loosen a region’s rule set, or switch a mode off in a region at its quiet hours so that its players gather in the modes that remain. Both are a mode write, effective within a second.

What to measure

  • The wait until a game server, per pool: assigned_at minus created_at, at the median and at 95%. These are the two numbers of NFR3. matched_at minus created_at shows how much of that wait is forming and how much is the fleet.
  • The age of the oldest waiting ticket, per pool: the smallest created_at among waiting tickets. It rises before the percentiles do.
  • The share of tickets that expire, per pool and hour, from end_reason. A rising share in one pool means its rules are too strict for its population.
  • The age of the oldest reserving match. Over 10 s means assigners are not keeping up or are down.
  • Matches dropped, by drop_reason, and tickets with requeues above 0: the fleet’s health as players feel it.
  • Pools with tickets waiting and no match formed for 10 s: an owner that is stuck, or a rule set no ticket can meet.

The store’s main machine is lost at the evening peak

The primary of the busiest region’s store fails at 20:00:00. The region is receiving 400 joins a second.

  1. 20:00:00. Commits stop. Joins are answered with “try again”. Owners’ rounds fail at their first statement, so they form nothing; renewals fail too, and the leases will run out, which does not matter because nobody can take them either. Assigners cannot claim. Streams stay open and report nothing new.
  2. Up to 20:01:00. The standby that has received more of the primary’s log is promoted. This design allows 60 s for it; how long it really takes depends on the failover manager and its settings.
  3. The store is back. Every ticket that was acknowledged is there, with its waiting time. The other standby follows the new primary, so every commit is on two machines again from the first one on. A node takes each pool’s lease in its next check. Matches that were being committed at the failure either committed whole or not at all; the ones that did not are formed again in the first round. Assigners pick up every reserving match; those past their deadline are dropped and their tickets are first in line.
  4. The held-back joins return.
joins held back: 60 s × 400 = 24,000 joins
returning over 10 s: 24,000 ÷ 10 s = 2,400 joins/s

Clients spread their retries over 10 s, so the region sees 2,400 joins a second on top of its normal 400: 2,800 a second, a little more than the event-start rate it is sized for and well inside the 12,800 its connections can carry. The first rounds after the failover read a larger pool, at most the 24,000 held-back tickets shared among three pools, and work through it oldest first.

Players who were waiting through the outage waited up to 60 s longer; that time counts towards their limits, so their first round after the failover has the widest candidates. Nothing was lost and nothing was matched twice.

Design at lower scale: one process per region

Below about 100,000 players online in a region, you can drop the lease per pool, the separate assigners and the open streams. One matchmaker process per region forms the matches and reserves the servers, with one spare behind a single lease, and clients read their ticket once a second. The 100,000 is the size at which one database connection, committing 100 matches a second, still covers an event start.

What stays is everything the guarantees rest on: the store with its standbys, the condition in every write, and the rules.

The working runs the estimates backwards: 8 tickets to a match, an event start of 6 times the evening peak, 1.25 players to a ticket and one join every 600 s. The simple design stops being enough when an event start needs more than 100 match commits a second, or when pausing all of a region’s pools for up to 8 s at a takeover is too much. The step up is small, because nothing stored changes: more leases, more connections, and the assigner loop moved into its own process.
